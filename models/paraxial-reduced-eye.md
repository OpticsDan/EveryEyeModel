# Paraxial (reduced) eye

A single ideal lens of about 60 dioptres standing in for the whole eye.

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Power | 60 D |
| Focal length | 16.67 mm (1000 / 60) |
| Medium on both sides | air (n = 1) in this form, so the image forms in air |
| Aberrations | none |
| Surfaces that reflect | none |

## How to enter it

- In most design programs: one paraxial (ideal) surface with focal length 16.67 mm, then
  16.67 mm of air to the image plane, which stands for the retina.
- Put the stop at the paraxial surface, sized for the pupil you are studying.

## What it is good for

First-order layout, pupil positions and magnification, quickly. With no aberrations and no
surfaces, it cannot show the eye's own blur or the reflections from the cornea and lens. Switch to a
schematic eye for those.

## Variants

Reduced eyes in the textbooks usually keep a single refracting surface and fill the eye with a
medium of index about 4/3, so that the image forms inside the eye at the right distance. The power
stays near 60 D either way. Use the air form above for the simplest layout work, or a single-surface
form when you need the image-space index.

## Source

The 60 D paraxial eye is the standard first-order stand-in for the eye; see any visual optics
text, for example D. A. Atchison and G. Smith, *Optics of the Human Eye* (Butterworth-Heinemann,
2000), on reduced eyes.
